# Chapter 4: Preamble Writing - SCQA Narrative Structure

## What is a Preamble?

The preamble is the opening section that frames the main point. It uses storytelling (narrative) structure to lead readers from what they know to the question your document will answer.

**Purpose:** Ensure readers understand WHY your answer matters before you deliver it.

## The SCQA Framework

SCQA stands for: Situation-Complication-Question-Answer

### 1. Situation (S)

**What it is:** Context that readers already understand; the stable state before things changed

**Characteristics:**
- Known facts (market conditions, company policies, historical performance)
- No surprise; reader likely agrees with this
- Sets stage for what comes next
- Usually present tense or historical present

**Examples:**
- "For 20 years, our industry operated with three major competitors"
- "The company has focused on enterprise customers exclusively"
- "Interest rates have remained stable at 3-4%"

**NOT situation:**
- "We're losing money" (this is a problem, not stable context)
- "The team is demoralized" (this is a complication, not background)

### 2. Complication (C)

**What it is:** New event or change that disrupts the situation and creates a problem

**Characteristics:**
- Recent or emerging (not old news)
- Unexpected or significant shift
- Creates implied question in reader's mind
- Uses words like: "However," "Suddenly," "Recently," "Now," "But then"

**Examples:**
- "But in the last 18 months, three new well-funded competitors entered the market"
- "Then the pandemic forced us to go remote, and customer expectations shifted"
- "A startup with breakthrough technology arrived with ample capital"

**The key:** Complication must RAISE A QUESTION in reader's mind

### 3. Question (Q)

**What it is:** The problem or issue that the complication creates; what readers now need to know

**Characteristics:**
- Implied or explicit (stated as "So we must decide..." or left implicit)
- Flows directly from the complication
- Reader should feel "Yes, that's exactly what I want to know"
- The CORE question your document answers

**Examples:**
- (Implied) "If three new competitors entered, how do we respond?"
- (Explicit) "We must decide: should we go fully remote or hybrid?"
- (Implied) "Does a startup with new technology threaten our position?"

**Critical insight:** If your answer doesn't directly address this question, your preamble is wrong.

### 4. Answer (A)

**What it is:** Your main point; the conclusion/recommendation your document supports

**Characteristics:**
- Directly answers the question raised by the complication
- Usually one sentence
- Should feel like logical response to the situation + complication + question
- This becomes the apex of your pyramid

**Examples:**
- Answer: "We should focus on vertical integration rather than competing on price"
- Answer: "We should adopt a permanent remote-first culture"
- Answer: "We should partner with the startup rather than compete"

## The SCQA Narrative Arc

Think of it as a story:

```
SITUATION: "Here's what was true..."
         ↓
COMPLICATION: "Then something changed..."
         ↓
QUESTION: "Which creates this problem..."
         ↓
ANSWER: "And here's what we should do about it"
```

The reader follows a logical path from agreement (situation) to curiosity (complication/question) to interest (wanting to see your answer).

## Common SCQA Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Opportunity

**Situation:** Market was stable, we had our share
**Complication:** New technology/market opened up
**Question:** Should we pursue this new opportunity?
**Answer:** Yes, we should [specific action]

*Example:* "Three Asian economies have opened markets. Should we enter? Yes, because..."

### Pattern 2: The Threat

**Situation:** We were successful with our current approach
**Complication:** New competitor/technology/regulation threatens our model
**Question:** How do we respond?
**Answer:** We should [defensive/offensive action]

*Example:* "We've led with enterprise software. A startup offers cheaper alternative. Should we respond? Yes, by..."

### Pattern 3: The Inefficiency

**Situation:** We're doing work the traditional way
**Complication:** New process/tool could improve efficiency
**Question:** Should we adopt the new approach?
**Answer:** Yes, we should implement it because...

*Example:* "We handle customer service manually. AI chatbots could help. Should we deploy? Yes, when we..."

### Pattern 4: The Mismatch

**Situation:** We set a goal/strategy
**Complication:** Current execution doesn't align with that goal
**Question:** What adjustments are needed?
**Answer:** We should [specific changes]

*Example:* "We committed to 30% growth. Current initiatives will only deliver 15%. How do we close the gap? We should..."

### Pattern 5: The Uncertain Hypothesis

**Situation:** We believe something should be true
**Complication:** We haven't validated whether it actually is
**Question:** Is our assumption correct?
**Answer:** Our research shows [yes/no], so we should...

*Example:* "We assume customers want feature X. We tested with 50 customers. Do they? Yes, and strongly."

## Writing a Strong Preamble

### Step 1: Identify Your Core Question

Before writing SCQA, ask yourself: "What question does my document answer?"

This is the QUESTION component. Everything else builds to it.

### Step 2: Work Backwards to Situation

What context must readers know to understand why this question matters?

- Economic conditions?
- Industry changes?
- Company strategy?
- Historical performance?

Keep situation SHORT. 1-2 sentences usually suffice.

### Step 3: Bridge with Complication

What changed between the situation and now?

The complication makes the question NECESSARY. Without it, readers wouldn't need your answer.

Make complication SPECIFIC. "Market changed" is vague. "Market consolidated from 5 players to 2" is specific.

### Step 4: State or Imply the Question

Make readers feel the tension between situation and complication. This creates curiosity about the answer.

Can be explicit: "The question is: should we enter this market?"
Or implied: Readers will naturally wonder "So what do we do?"

### Step 5: Deliver the Answer

State your main point clearly. It should feel like a natural resolution to the situation-complication-question arc.

## Length Guidelines

- Situation: 1-2 sentences (30-50 words)
- Complication: 1-2 sentences (30-50 words)
- Question: Implicit or 1 sentence (0-20 words)
- Answer: 1 sentence (15-25 words)

**Total preamble:** 3-5 sentences, 75-150 words

This is SHORT. It frames the full pyramid without lengthy explanation.

## Common Preamble Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Missing Complication
"Market is growing. Therefore, we should expand."
- Problem: No tension; reader doesn't feel urgency
- Fix: Add complication: "Market is growing BUT new competitors are entering"

### Mistake 2: Weak Situation
"Things are changing a lot in our industry"
- Problem: Too vague; reader unclear what situation actually is
- Fix: Be specific: "Our customer base is 80% Fortune 500 companies"

### Mistake 3: Long Situation
Three paragraphs of background and history
- Problem: Readers forget what the situation was before you get to complication
- Fix: Compress to 1-2 sentences; use appendix for detailed history

### Mistake 4: Answer Before Question
"We should go direct-to-consumer because margins improve by 20%. But first, let me explain the market..."
- Problem: You've spoiled the story; reader tunes out before question
- Fix: Follow SCQA order; answer only after stating question

### Mistake 5: Wrong Complication Level
"Market grew 5% last year BUT market grew 6% this year"
- Problem: Both are positive; this isn't actually a complication
- Fix: Find the actual complication: "Market grew BUT pricing pressure is increasing" or "Market grew BUT new entrants gaining share"

### Mistake 6: Question Doesn't Match Answer
- Situation: Pricing pressure
- Complication: Competitors cut price 20%
- Question: How do we survive price competition?
- Answer: We should invest in R&D and innovation
- Problem: Answer doesn't address the question raised (price competition)
- Fix: Answer should address competition specifically

## SCQA in Different Formats

### Executive Summary Preamble (1 paragraph)
Compress all four elements into 4-5 sentences.

### Email Preamble (2 sentences)
Ultra-short: Situation + Complication/Question, then your answer in next sentence.

### Presentation Opening (1 slide)
Situation, Complication, Question as narrative flow. Answer on next slide(s).

### Long Report Preamble (1-2 pages)
Can expand each element to multiple sentences while maintaining SCQA flow.

## Testing Your Preamble

**The Clarity Test:** Can someone who knows nothing about this topic understand WHY your answer matters?

**The Engagement Test:** Does the preamble make readers think "Yes, I want to know how you'll answer this"?

**The Alignment Test:** Does your answer directly address the question raised by the preamble?

If you fail any test, rewrite the preamble.

---

**Key Takeaway**: SCQA is a narrative formula that leads readers from what they know, through a problem, to the question your answer addresses. It makes your point feel necessary, not arbitrary.
