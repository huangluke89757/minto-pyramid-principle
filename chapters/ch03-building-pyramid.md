# Chapter 3: Building a Pyramid - Top-Down and Bottom-Up

## The Two Construction Methods

You can build a pyramid from the top (if you know your point) or from the bottom (if you're discovering your point).

## Method 1: Top-Down (The Direct Approach)

**Use when:** You already know your main point/conclusion

**Steps:**

1. **State the main point clearly** - Write one sentence that expresses your conclusion, recommendation, or finding
2. **Ask what must be true** - For your point to be valid, what would readers need to accept as true?
3. **List 2-4 supporting reasons** - Answer the above question
4. **Verify grouping logic** - Do they share a common principle? Are they mutually exclusive?
5. **Add evidence** - For each reason, list the evidence that supports it
6. **Organize into levels** - Create the full pyramid structure
7. **Write the preamble** - Create SCQA narrative to lead into the main point

**Example:**

- **Main point:** "We should enter the Southeast Asian market"
- **What must be true?** Customers want it, we can deliver it, it's profitable
- **Three reasons:**
  - Market fundamentals are attractive (size, growth, affluence)
  - We have competitive advantages (cost structure, technology)
  - Execution is feasible (team, timeline, capital)
- **Evidence for each:**
  - Fundamentals: $2B market, 12% CAGR, median income rising 8% annually
  - Advantages: 30% lower manufacturing costs than regional competitors, proprietary tech
  - Feasibility: Hired regional VP, can launch in 6 months with $5M

**Advantage:** Fast when you know where you're headed

**Disadvantage:** Risk of having weak point if you haven't done the analysis

## Method 2: Bottom-Up (The Discovery Approach)

**Use when:** You have information/data but don't yet have a clear conclusion

**Steps:**

1. **Collect all relevant information** - List facts, data points, observations
2. **Cluster related items** - Group into natural categories
3. **Create summary for each cluster** - What do these items have in common?
4. **Look for overarching pattern** - What's the big idea these clusters point to?
5. **Test the conclusion** - Does it logically follow from the supporting clusters?
6. **Organize into pyramid** - Arrange into hierarchical structure
7. **Write the preamble** - Add SCQA framing

**Example:**

Facts collected:
- Customer A said "feature X would help us"
- Customer B asked for feature X
- Customer C said feature X is their top priority
- Competitor 1 offers feature X
- Competitor 2 offers feature X
- Our implementation cost: $2M
- Revenue potential: $10M+ annually
- Team can build in 4 months

**Clustering:**

- Customer demand cluster: A, B, C all want feature X
- Competitive pressure cluster: Both competitors offer it
- Business case cluster: Good ROI, fast timeline

**Summary for each:**
- Customer demand is strong (3/10 recent conversations mentioned it)
- Competitive landscape requires it (2/3 competitors have it)
- Business viability is solid ($10M potential, $2M cost, 4-month timeline)

**Overarching pattern:**
- We should build feature X (implied by all three clusters)

**Full structure:**
- **Main point:** "We should prioritize Feature X development"
  - Strong customer demand justifies investment
  - Competitive necessity (both major competitors offer it)
  - Excellent business viability (10:1 ROI, fast delivery)

**Advantage:** More thorough; you've actually examined the evidence

**Disadvantage:** Takes more time; requires organizing messy data first

## Method 3: The Iterative Approach

In practice, most real work uses both methods:

1. **Start top-down** with hypothesis (I think we should do X)
2. **Test bottom-up** by gathering evidence
3. **Refine top-down** based on what evidence reveals
4. **Iterate** until convinced

## Testing Your Pyramid

### The Spouse Test

Can you explain your pyramid to someone not familiar with the topic in 2 minutes using only the top two levels?

If no, your structure is unclear.

**Example:**
- Main point: "We should enter Southeast Asia"
- Three reasons: "Strong market fundamentals, we have advantages, we can execute"
- If spouse says "Got it," you pass the test
- If they ask "What advantages?" or "But what about competition?" you need to clarify

### The Sufficiency Test

Does your pyramid prove the point, or just support it?

**Not sufficient:**
- "We should buy Company X because we want to grow"
- (Why growth matters and why acquisition is the way aren't proven)

**Sufficient:**
- "We should buy Company X because: it gives us market access we need, we can afford it, integration risk is low"
- (Each reason proves necessity AND feasibility)

### The Logic Test

Flip each level horizontally and see if items really are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.

**Question:** "Why should we enter Southeast Asia?"

**Pyramid says:**
- Market fundamentals are attractive
- We have competitive advantages
- Execution is feasible

**Flip test:**
- If market fundamentals weren't attractive, would we still enter? No
- If we had no advantages, would we still enter? No
- If execution wasn't feasible, would we still enter? No
- Is anything missing? No - covers why entry makes sense

**Pass:** This is a solid set

## Common Structure Problems and Fixes

### Problem 1: Weak Point
"We should improve our software"
- Too vague; doesn't specify what improvement or software
- Fix: "We should invest $2M in modernizing our legacy CRM system"

### Problem 2: Unequal Support
- Point: "We should expand"
- Reason 1: "Strong market demand with 3 pages of evidence"
- Reason 2: "Product fits market" with weak explanation
- Reason 3: "Team is capable" with moderate evidence
- Fix: Either strengthen reason 2 & 3, or promote reason 1 to main point and lower others

### Problem 3: Hidden Assumptions
- Point: "We should go direct-to-consumer"
- Support: "Margin improvement of 20%, reduces customer acquisition time"
- Reader question: "But what about brand dilution?" "Distribution logistics?"
- Fix: Add supporting point addressing concerns: "We can maintain brand through strategic partnerships" or acknowledge as risk

### Problem 4: Overly Broad Support
- Point: "We should update our technology stack"
- Support 1: "Current stack is outdated"
- Support 2: "Team wants newer technology"
- Support 3: "We'll attract better talent"
- Problem: Mixed grouping (problems, wants, benefits)
- Fix: Group: "Necessity (outdated, talent competition) and Opportunity (team development)"

### Problem 5: Too Many Levels
- Pyramid goes 6+ levels deep
- Readers lose the forest for the trees
- Fix: Limit to 3-4 levels maximum; if deeper, create multiple pyramids

## When You Get Stuck

**If you can't find your point:**
1. What decision do I need readers to make?
2. What do I want them to believe?
3. What action do I want them to take?

**If your support doesn't seem enough:**
1. Do I actually have a strong point?
2. Should I demote it to a supporting reason?
3. Do I need different evidence?

**If structure feels forced:**
1. Are these really the 2-4 core ideas?
2. Should I regroup these under a different principle?
3. Is the grouping principle clear to readers?

---

**Key Takeaway**: Top-down works when you know your point; bottom-up works when discovering it. Most real work combines both.
