# Chapter 11: PowerPoint Presentation of Pyramid Structure

## Core Idea

Translate pyramid structure into visual slides. Each slide contains ONE idea; the slide deck as a whole tells the complete story through visual hierarchy and logic flow.

## Frameworks Introduced

### 1. **The "One Idea Per Slide" Rule (一屏一观点)**

**Principle:**
- Thesis slide: Your central recommendation (1 slide)
- Supporting slides: 2-4 reasons why (1 slide each)
- Evidence slides: Data proving each reason (1+ slides each)
- Total structure mirrors pyramid

**Example Structure:**
```
Slide 1: "Implement Three-Part Quality Program" (Thesis)
  ↓
Slide 2: "Preventive maintenance restores 50% of production" (Reason 1)
Slide 3: "Testing capacity expansion restores 30%" (Reason 2)
Slide 4: "Quality improvement restores 20%" (Reason 3)
  ↓
Slides 5-6: Evidence for Reason 1 (charts, data)
Slides 7-8: Evidence for Reason 2
Slides 9-10: Evidence for Reason 3
  ↓
Slide 11: "ROI Summary: 400% in 18 months" (Conclusion)
```

### 2. **Hierarchy of Slide Types**

**Slide 1: Title/Thesis Slide**
- One sentence stating your main recommendation
- Background (company name, date, your name)
- Not a detailed agenda
- Purpose: Announce the answer to the reader's question

**Slides 2-N: Supporting Argument Slides**
- Each contains one supporting point
- Title states the point (not just a topic)
- Visual (chart, diagram, or simple text) supports the title
- Reader grasps point in 5 seconds of reading

**Example Good Title:**
- "Preventive maintenance can restore 50% production"
- NOT: "Maintenance Issues" or "Production Problems"

**Data/Evidence Slides**
- Show specific data supporting the argument
- Charts > Tables (visual scanning faster)
- Label clearly so meaning is obvious without explanation
- Detailed backup for claims made in argument slides

**Transition Slides**
- Between major sections (optional but recommended)
- State what you've covered, what's next
- Maintains reader orientation in longer presentations

**Conclusion Slide**
- Restates thesis
- May add "next steps" section

### 3. **Visual Design Principles**

**The "Glance & Grasp" Standard:**
Reader should understand slide meaning in one glance (5 seconds max)

**Text Guidelines:**
- Maximum 5 bullets per slide
- Each bullet ≤ 12 words
- Font size ≥ 24pt (readable from distance)
- Title always conveys the claim

**Chart Guidelines:**
- One chart per slide (or closely related pair)
- Clearly labeled axes, legend
- Color coding is optional (not required)
- Avoid 3D effects, excessive decoration

**Color & Consistency:**
- Consistent background throughout
- Typography consistent (same fonts, sizing)
- Color palette limited (3-5 colors)
- High contrast (black on white, dark on light)

## Mental Model: The Slide-to-Argument Mapping

```
Audience thinking:
"Do I believe this slide?" → "Is this slide proven?" → "Does this answer my question?"
                    ↓                        ↓                      ↓
            Argument slide         Evidence slide        Conclusion slide
            (Claim)                (Proof)               (Recommendation)
```

## Worked Example

**Bad Presentation (No Clear Structure):**
```
Slide 1: "Production Analysis" [shows mixed data]
Slide 2: "Downtime Trends" [more data]
Slide 3: "Labor Metrics" [different data]
Slide 4: "Quality Issues" [yet more data]
Slide 5: "Conclusions"
```
Viewer is confused: "What's the point? What should I do?"

**Good Presentation (Pyramid Structure):**
```
Slide 1: Thesis
"We recommend implementing preventive maintenance,
testing expansion, and quality improvements"

Slide 2: Reason 1
"Preventive maintenance will restore 50% of lost production"
(Shows: Production downtime trending up 40%, 
maintenance hours declining 20%)

Slide 3: Reason 2
"Testing capacity expansion will restore 30% of lost production"
(Shows: Testing department at capacity, queue time up 3 weeks)

Slide 4: Reason 3
"Quality improvement will restore 20% of lost production"
(Shows: Scrap rate up 40%, rework hours doubled)

Slide 5: Implementation Timeline
(Gantt chart showing 3-month rollout)

Slide 6: Financial Summary
(Shows ROI: 400% payback in 18 months)

Slide 7: Next Steps
"Q3: Launch pilot in Plant A, Q4: Full rollout if successful"
```

Viewer immediately understands: The problem is multi-faceted, here's the three-part solution, here's proof it works and here's the timeline.

## Key Takeaways

1. **One idea per slide** — Each slide = one thought = one argument element
2. **Title states the claim** — Not just topic, but complete argument
3. **Support is visual** — Charts > text; visuals support claims
4. **Consistency matters** — Same fonts, colors, styles throughout
5. **Glance-and-grasp rule** — 5-second comprehension threshold
6. **No hidden slides** — Everything needed to prove argument appears in sequence
7. **Slide order = argument order** — Follows pyramid top-down
8. **Less text more** — Minimal words, maximal visuals

## Anti-Patterns

- Don't create slides with mixed messages (title says X, chart shows Y)
- Don't use bullets to explain charts (let chart speak)
- Don't create "data dump" slides (overwhelming detail)
- Don't break pyramid logic in sequence (random order confuses)
- Don't hide key points in detailed slides (lead with conclusion)
- Don't over-decorate (animations, clipart, busy backgrounds)
- Don't assume audience will "read ahead" — present sequentially

## Design Checklist

- [ ] Thesis slide clearly states recommendation
- [ ] Each supporting slide has one argument
- [ ] Slide title expresses the argument (not just topic)
- [ ] Visuals (charts/diagrams) support titles
- [ ] Text minimal (≤ 12 words per bullet)
- [ ] Font sizes readable (≥ 24pt)
- [ ] Color scheme consistent throughout
- [ ] Sequence follows pyramid structure (top-down)
- [ ] Evidence slides provide proof for claims
- [ ] Conclusion summarizes and calls to action
